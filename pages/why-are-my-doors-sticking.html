STICKING DOORS | RESOURCE 05

Why Are My Doors Sticking?

A door that suddenly becomes hard to open, drags across the floor, or no longer latches is telling you that something changed. The change may be limited to the hinges, door, or frame. It may also involve moisture, the floor below the opening, or movement in the surrounding wall and structure. The useful first step is not to trim the door. It is to find where the fit changed and decide whether the problem stops at the opening or continues into the building around it.

Start With the Exact Place the Door Sticks

Open and close the door slowly while watching the narrow space between the slab and frame. That space is the reveal. Note whether the door catches at the top corner, rubs along the latch side, drags at the floor, or moves freely but misses the strike plate. If the problem is limited to the opening itself, window and door repair work may eventually correct it, but the contact pattern should be understood first.

The location of the contact does not prove the cause. It does give you a useful starting point and a way to tell whether an adjustment actually corrects the problem or only moves it somewhere else.

The top corner rubs

The door may be sagging at the hinges, the frame may be out of square, or the opening may have changed shape.

The bottom drags

Check the hinge side, threshold, floor covering, and whether the floor elevation changed near the opening.

The latch misses

The door and strike plate are no longer aligned. Record whether the latch now lands above, below, or beside the opening.

The entire edge is tight

Paint buildup, swelling, warping, installation clearance, or movement in the frame may be involved.

Check the Simple Parts Before Blaming the Structure

Look closely at the hinges. Tighten nothing until you first note whether screws are loose, a hinge has pulled away, or the door drops when you gently lift the handle. Check the strike plate, weatherstripping, threshold, and painted edges for an obvious point of interference.

Casing, jambs, stops, and other finish pieces can also loosen or shift. If the issue is confined to those components, trim carpentry may be the appropriate repair rather than work on the structure below.

Look for loose or missing hinge screws.
Check whether a hinge leaf has separated from the door or jamb.
Note where the latch contacts the strike plate.
Look for thick paint, damaged weatherstripping, debris, or a raised threshold.
Check whether new flooring or a transition now contacts the door.

Pay Attention to When the Problem Happens

A wood door can fit more tightly when its moisture content changes with the surrounding air. The USDA Wood Handbook explains that wood in service continues to undergo moisture changes and that seasonal variation can produce dimensional change. That makes timing useful evidence.

A door that sticks during humid weather but works normally during a drier period may be reacting differently from a door that became tight after a leak or continues to worsen in every season. Write down when the problem appears instead of relying on one moment.

Does the door change after rain or during humid weather?
Is the condition seasonal or present every day?
Did it begin after a leak, storm, repair, or flooring installation?
Is the contact point staying the same or spreading along the edge?

Do Not Trim the Door Before You Understand the Change

Sanding, planing, or cutting material from the door may make it close today. It may also erase the contact pattern and leave an excessive gap later if the condition was temporary.

Before altering the slab, photograph the reveal on all four sides, the exact place it contacts the frame or floor, and the position of the latch against the strike plate. If an adjustment is made, record what changed so the result can be evaluated instead of guessed.

One Sticking Door Is Different From Several

One door with a loose hinge can be a local repair. Several doors or windows changing in the same part of the building provide a different pattern. Compare their reveals, latch alignment, and the surrounding walls and trim.

When more than one opening has changed, a broader building inspection and diagnostic assessment can evaluate the openings in relation to the floor, wall, framing, moisture, and support conditions instead of treating every door as an unrelated problem.

Check other doors and windows in the same room or hallway.
Look for trim joints that opened or shifted.
Note cracks near corners of doors and windows.
Compare whether problems appear on one level or across several areas.

Look at the Floor Beneath the Opening

A door frame is connected to the wall, and the wall is supported by the floor and structure below. If the nearby floor slopes, dips, or feels soft, read Why Is My Floor Uneven? and compare that floor pattern with the change at the doorway.

Movement below the opening may eventually require framing repairs or foundation repair and house leveling, but those are conclusions to support with property evidence, not assumptions to make from a sticking door alone.

Does the door swing open or closed on its own?
Is the gap above the door wider at one end?
Does the floor change elevation across the doorway?
Does the floor feel soft, springy, or different near the jamb?

Moisture Can Affect the Door and the Opening Around It

Moisture should not be reduced to the idea that the door simply swelled. The slab, jamb, wall framing, subfloor, trim, and nearby flooring can respond differently depending on where water entered and how long materials stayed damp.

At an exterior opening, review drainage, weather exposure, flashing, thresholds, and nearby materials. When water around the building may be influencing the support or wall conditions, foundation, drainage, and waterproofing work should be considered in the context of the whole pathway rather than as a generic answer.

Look for staining, peeling finishes, swollen trim, or softened material.
Check whether the problem follows rain or wet weather.
Note musty odor or changes in nearby flooring.
Inspect accessible exterior drainage and the bottom of the opening without removing materials.

Preserve the Pattern Before Repairs Begin

A short record helps separate a one-time hardware issue from a building condition that is changing. It also gives the person inspecting the property something more useful than the statement that the door sometimes sticks.

Photograph the full door and frame from both sides.
Photograph the reveal and exact contact point.
Record when the problem began and whether it is worsening.
Note recent weather, leaks, repairs, or floor work.
Check and record other affected doors and windows.
Mark whether the latch lands high, low, or to one side.

When a Door Adjustment May Be Enough

A localized adjustment may be appropriate when the evidence points to a clear, limited condition such as loose hardware, a displaced strike plate, damaged weatherstripping, paint buildup, or inadequate installation clearance.

The repair should match the condition found. Tightening a hinge is different from correcting a warped jamb. Moving a strike plate is different from repairing moisture-damaged material. Trimming a door is different from correcting movement below the opening.

When the Opening Deserves a Broader Inspection

A broader evaluation makes sense when several openings change, the frame appears out of square, the nearby floor has changed, cracks or separated trim are developing, moisture evidence is present, or repeated adjustments do not hold.

The goal is not to attach a frightening label to the symptom. It is to identify what moved, why the clearances changed, whether the condition is isolated or connected to other parts of the building, and what work should happen first.

More than one door or window has changed.
The sticking continues to worsen.
The floor near the opening slopes, sags, or feels soft.
Cracks or trim separations are developing around the opening.
Moisture staining or swelling is present.
The condition followed a storm, leak, impact, or construction work.
Repeated adjustments have not held.

The Right Repair Follows the Diagnosis

The answer is not always to repair the foundation, and it is not always to trim the door. The right repair may involve hardware, the door or frame, windows and doors, finish carpentry, moisture correction, flooring, framing, drainage, or structural support. The scope should follow the condition that can actually be documented.
