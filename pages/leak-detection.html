Find the Source Before Repairing the Damage

Water can travel away from its source before it becomes visible. A ceiling stain may begin at a roof opening, plumbing connection, HVAC drain, or another location above or beside the damaged area. Water beneath flooring may come from plumbing, an appliance, an exterior opening, groundwater, or moisture moving through connected materials.

Leak detection investigates those conditions rather than selecting a repair from the visible symptom alone. The investigation considers where the water appears, when it occurs, which systems are nearby, how materials are connected, and where moisture has traveled.

The objective is to identify the active source or contributing conditions, document what is affected, and define the correct next service.

Conditions We Investigate

AC or HVAC Leaking Water

Condensate drains, drain pans, equipment conditions, airflow, insulation, temperature differences, and nearby building materials may contribute to water around an HVAC system.

Pipe and Plumbing Leaks

Supply piping, drain piping, fittings, fixtures, water heaters, appliances, and concealed plumbing can release water into walls, ceilings, cabinets, flooring, crawlspaces, and mechanical areas.

Roof Leaks

Roof coverings, flashing, penetrations, valleys, edges, drainage, damaged decking, and storm-related openings can allow water to move through attics, insulation, ceilings, and walls.

Window and Door Water Entry

Openings can admit water at flashing, sealant joints, sill areas, thresholds, trim, siding transitions, weather barriers, and adjoining wall assemblies.

Water under Flooring

Water beneath tile, wood, laminate, carpet, underlayment, or subflooring may originate from plumbing, appliances, exterior entry, slab conditions, or another concealed source.

Wet Walls and Ceilings

Stains, damaged finishes, soft materials, peeling coatings, or recurring dampness may record water moving inside a wall or ceiling cavity.

Crawlspace Water

Plumbing leakage, groundwater, surface drainage, foundation entry, condensation, HVAC conditions, or discharge problems can create water and humidity beneath a building.

Basement Water

Foundation walls, floor joints, penetrations, cracks, below-grade drainage, grading, groundwater, plumbing, and waterproofing conditions may contribute to basement water.

Recurring Moisture with No Obvious Source

Repeated stains, odors, damp materials, microbial growth, or finish damage may indicate an unresolved source, intermittent leak, condensation condition, or connected building-system problem.

Leak Sources Can Cross Building Systems

A water problem may involve more than one system. Roof runoff may enter through an opening, travel inside a wall, wet insulation and framing, reach electrical areas, and appear at flooring below. An HVAC condensate problem may affect equipment, ducts, walls, ceilings, or nearby finishes. A plumbing leak may enter a crawlspace or concealed floor assembly before appearing in an occupied room.

Leak Investigation Process

1. Document the Reported Condition

Record where the water appears, when it was first noticed, whether it changes during rainfall or equipment operation, and whether previous repairs have been attempted.

2. Inspect Accessible Areas

Review visible damage, adjacent rooms, exterior conditions, roof and drainage relationships, mechanical areas, plumbing locations, crawlspaces, basements, and relevant accessible concealed spaces.

3. Identify Possible Sources

Separate plumbing, HVAC condensation, roof entry, window or door entry, surface drainage, groundwater, foundation seepage, and other possible sources according to property evidence.

4. Trace the Water Path

Evaluate how water may move through connected materials, cavities, floors, ceilings, walls, insulation, framing, finishes, and building systems.

5. Document Affected Materials and Systems

Record visible damage, moisture conditions, affected assemblies, equipment relationships, contents exposure, and areas that may require additional access or testing.

6. Define the Next Service

Identify whether the next work belongs under Plumbing, HVAC, Roofing, Waterproofing, Windows and Doors, Crawlspace Encapsulation, Water Damage Restoration, or another verified repair path.

Leak Detection and Water Damage Restoration Are Different Stages

Leak Detection investigates where water originated, how it traveled, which systems are involved, and what should happen next.

Water Damage Restoration addresses damage that has already occurred. That work may include emergency extraction, mitigation, containment, cleaning, drying, moisture monitoring, material removal, contents care, repair, and reconstruction.

A property may need one service or both. The source should be addressed so restoration is not completed while the condition that caused the damage remains active.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you find a leak hidden behind a wall or ceiling?

The investigation begins with visible evidence and accessible property conditions. Concealed areas may require additional access, testing, or selective opening when the source or water path cannot be established from accessible areas alone.

Why is water showing in a different place from the leak?

Water can move along framing, piping, ducts, fasteners, insulation, flooring, roof assemblies, wall cavities, and other connected materials before becoming visible.

Do you investigate AC or HVAC systems leaking water?

Yes. The investigation can include condensate drains, drain pans, equipment areas, airflow, insulation, temperature conditions, and affected building materials. Verified HVAC repairs are coordinated through the HVAC service path.

Is leak detection the same as water damage restoration?

No. Leak detection investigates the source and path. Water Damage Restoration addresses affected materials through mitigation, cleaning, drying, documentation, repair, and reconstruction.

Can a roof or window leak look like a plumbing leak?

Yes. Different water sources can produce similar stains or damp areas. Rainfall, plumbing use, HVAC operation, exterior openings, roof conditions, and possible water paths must be evaluated before selecting a repair.

What information should I record before the inspection?

If it is safe, record where and when the water appears, whether it changes during rain or equipment use, photographs of visible conditions, prior repairs, and changes in stains, odors, flooring, ceilings, walls, crawlspaces, or mechanical areas.
